Reading the flow off the wall: near-wall velocity from wall shear stress, and how far up it works

From a line of wall shear stress, a 1-D U-Net predicts the streamwise velocity at y+ = 5 and 10 in a turbulent channel with errors of 0.19 and 0.39 of the target's spread on snapshots it has never seen. The original comparison put it a thousand times ahead of the wall law; the law had lost its viscosity and one wall's sign, and written correctly the margin is 2 to 3 times. Ridge regression on the same input is close behind, a random split flatters both by 10 to 17 %, and by y+ = 30 nothing beats predicting the mean: the wall informs the sublayer and the lower buffer layer, and that is all.

Objective

The wall is the one place in a turbulent flow where a sensor is easy to put and a simulation is hardest to resolve. If the velocity a few wall units above the surface could be read off the wall shear stress, a wall model or a sensor array could stand in for the finest part of the grid. This project asks how far up that works, with one network and the references that a careful engineer would try first. The work is two stages, each a notebook:

  1. Stage 1 — A 1-D U-Net from the wall shear stress to the velocity at y+ = 5 and 10. From 5,777 planes of a turbulent channel simulation, 11,554 lines of the wall gradient du/dy along x (two walls per plane) are mapped by a 1-D U-Net to the streamwise velocity at y+ ≈ 5 and 10 along the same line.
  2. Stage 2 — Height and generalisation: ridge, the wall law and three splits. The same question at five heights up to the channel centre, against ridge regression on the same input and the correct first-order wall law, and on three splits: the random one, later snapshots held out, and whole spanwise planes held out.

Every training is repeated with seeds 0, 1 and 2, seed 0 being the base run. The data are the same channel-flow planes as my U-Net project, so this repository points at that release rather than uploading them again.

Problem statement

Given τ_w(x), the wall-normal gradient of u at the wall along a line of 384 points, predict u(x) at a height y+ above that wall. Near the wall the answer is almost given away: in the viscous sublayer u grows linearly with the distance, so u ≈ (du/dy) · y is a reference that costs nothing. The question is what a network adds to that law, at what height the wall stops informing the flow, and whether the number depends on how the data are split.

Method

Wall units and the first-order law

The friction velocity u_τ = √(ν |du/dy|) sets the wall unit; y+ = y u_τ / ν, and Re_τ ≈ 125 for these planes. In the viscous sublayer (y+ below about 5) the velocity is linear in the distance, u+ = y+, which in the stored units reads u ≈ (du/dy) · (y − y_wall). The original notebook's baseline writes it as u = u_τ · y+ with u_τ = √|τ_w| and keeps the sign of the top wall's gradient, which leaves out the viscosity and flips the sign at one wall: that baseline is 13.6 and 27.2 in error at the two heights, about a thousand times the network's, and the comparison in the notebook rests on it. Stage 2 restores the law.

The network

The network sees a whole line at once. Its receptive field, measured by the gradient of one output point with respect to the input, spans 188 points, 94 on each side: half the line. The periodic direction x is zero-padded at the ends, so the error is largest at x = 0.

References, and three ways to hold data out

Stage 2 scores every model as the root-mean-square error divided by the target's own spread, so that 1 is the error of predicting the training mean and the heights can be compared. Ridge regression maps the 384 wall values to the 384 target values linearly; it needs the gradient taken into the fluid at both walls (the top wall multiplied by −1), because on the raw signed input it is no better than the mean. The first-order law and the training mean are the other references. The three splits are the random one of Stage 1, the last 20 % of snapshots held out whole, and the spanwise planes labelled 48, 49 and 80 to 89 held out, so that only one test plane has a training neighbour.

Which device

One training step of the U-Net inside model.fit takes 15.6 ms on the CPU and 81 ms on the Apple GPU (batch 32, median of 30 steps after 10 warm-up steps); the small reference networks are 5 to 6 times faster on the CPU too. Everything runs on the CPU, which the original notebook already assumed.

The U-Net is the best model at both heights, and its seed spread is tiny. But the margin is 2 to 3 times over the correct law, not a thousand, and a convolution block with 770 times fewer weights gets within 30 to 60 % of it. At y+ = 5 the law alone already beats the training mean by three times; at y+ = 10 it does not.

The two heights behave differently. At y+ = 5 the flow is inside the viscous sublayer, where the velocity really is linear in the distance from the wall, so the law is close and every learned model improves on it by a modest factor. At y+ = 10 the sublayer has ended, the law is worse than the mean, and the information the network extracts from the shape of the wall-gradient line along x is what makes the prediction possible at all.

The wall gradient informs the viscous sublayer and the lower buffer layer and little beyond. At y+ = 5 the U-Net removes 81 % of the target's spread and ridge 78 %; at 10 they remove 61 % and 52 %; at 15, 39 % and 29 %; at 30 the U-Net is within a few per cent of the mean predictor and ridge is at it; at the centre every model is worse than the mean. The network's advantage over a linear map is real but modest, about 0.03 to 0.1 of the spread, and it closes as the height grows.

The random split flatters both learned models by 10 to 17 %, because 95 % of its validation lines have a training neighbour one plane away, and neighbouring planes correlate at 0.90 to 0.98. The two honest splits agree with each other, which is the number to quote. One exception runs the other way: on the plane split at y+ = 30, ridge (0.927) beats every U-Net seed.

Stage 2's nine U-Nets were capped at 150 epochs and all reached the cap, so their errors are not converged by early stopping and may improve with longer training; ridge, the wall law and the mean are deterministic. The plane files' time attribute is not monotonic, which does not affect the results.

Key observations

  1. Check the baseline before the claim. The original comparison showed the network a thousand times better than the wall law; the law had lost its viscosity and one wall's sign. Written correctly, the network is 2 to 3 times better at y+ = 5 and 10.
  2. The wall informs about ten wall units of flow. The U-Net removes 81 % of the target's spread at y+ = 5, 61 % at 10, 39 % at 15 and almost nothing at 30; at the centre nothing beats the mean. A wall sensor is a sublayer sensor.
  3. A linear map gets most of the way. Ridge regression on the sign-corrected gradient is within 0.03 to 0.1 of the spread of the U-Net at every height, and ahead of it once on the plane split at y+ = 30. The network's gain is real, beyond the seed range up to y+ = 15, and modest.
  4. The split is part of the number. A random split over neighbouring planes flatters both learned models by 10 to 17 %; held-out snapshots and held-out planes agree with each other.
  5. Size is not the lever here. A single convolution block of 882 weights gets within 30 to 60 % of a U-Net of 677,000, and the U-Net's seed spread is below 1 % of its error: the problem is nearly linear and well determined, so the architecture buys little.
  6. Small references belong in every comparison. The notebook's own extensions, a dense network and a convolution block, place the U-Net's result on a scale that the broken law could not.

Conclusion

A 1-D U-Net reads the velocity at y+ = 5 and 10 off the wall shear stress with errors of 0.19 and 0.39 of the target's spread on snapshots it has never seen, better than a ridge regression on the same input by more than its seed range, and 2 to 3 times better than the correct first-order wall law, not the thousand times that the original baseline suggested. The information in the wall gradient runs out between y+ = 15 and 30, for the network and the linear map alike.

What the stages do not establish: whether the Stage 2 U-Nets, all stopped at the 150-epoch cap, would gain from longer training; whether two-dimensional input (the wall shear over a patch rather than a line) extends the useful height; and how the result scales with the Reynolds number, since only Re_τ ≈ 125 was available. The next steps are the Stage 2 trainings run to early stopping, a 2-D wall-patch input, and the same comparison with the spanwise velocity and pressure at the wall added to the input, which the sublayer law does not use.
