Physics in the loss: PINNs for forward and inverse heat problems, and a DeepONet for a whole family

One training loop asked three questions. Solve this field: a physics-informed network written by hand reproduces transient heat conduction to a few thousandths. Find this coefficient: one trainable number and twelve sensor readings recover a conductivity, when the readings allow it and the optimiser runs its course. Learn the whole family: a DeepONet trained on 1,500 solver runs maps any permeability field to its pressure in one pass.

Objective

A partial differential equation (PDE) relates a field, a temperature or a pressure, to its derivatives. The usual way to solve one is a grid solver. I wanted to find out, on problems whose answers I know, what a neural network that is given the equation can actually do instead, and for which question it earns its place. The work is five stages, each a notebook, built on one training loop:

  1. Stage 1 — A PINN for heat conduction, written by hand. A physics-informed neural network (PINN) written in plain PyTorch: transient heat conduction in a rod, then a cylindrical fuel pin and a two-dimensional plate, with six short studies and five experiments on what makes it work or fail.
  2. Stage 2 — The same recipe in DeepXDE, then turned into an inverse problem. The same recipe as library calls, a forward solve on a square, then the conductivity recovered from twelve sensor readings, plus a viscosity, two cases where the data cannot identify the unknown, and a moving solidification front.
  3. Stage 3 — The inverse problem by hand. The inverse idea in a few lines of PyTorch: λ in y'' + λy = 0 from four observations.
  4. Stage 4 — A DeepONet that learns a whole family of Darcy problems. A deep operator network (DeepONet) trained on 1,500 solver runs that maps any permeability field of a family to its pressure field in one pass.
  5. Stage 5 — Data efficiency and noise robustness across the stages. Two questions across the stages: how much data the operator needs, and how the library and the hand-written inverse PINN react to noisy readings.

Every trained result is repeated with three seeds, so that a difference is called an effect only when it is larger than the spread between repeats.

Problem statement

One loop runs through all five stages: a network, a loss, an optimiser. What changes is what the loss is built from and what comes out of it. With the equation and its boundary and initial conditions, the answer is one field. Add one trainable number and a few readings, and the answer is a field plus a coefficient, provided the readings determine it. Replace the equation by solved input–output pairs, and the answer is a network that serves a whole family of inputs.

No file passes between the stages: each builds its own points, readings or training pairs from code, and each one knows the true answer because it made its data from it. That makes every result checkable, and it is also the main limit: all readings are synthetic and consistent with the equation.

The questions: does a hand-written PINN reproduce a known field, and what settings decide whether it does? Can the same loop recover a material coefficient from a handful of sensors, and when can it not? What does a library add over a dozen lines of PyTorch? And when is it worth learning the whole map from input field to solution instead of solving one case at a time?

Method

The residual, from automatic differentiation

A small tanh network maps coordinates to a field. Automatic differentiation takes its derivatives with respect to those coordinates exactly; keeping the computation graph lets the loss be differentiated again with respect to the weights, through the derivatives. The equation, rearranged to read "this should be zero", is evaluated at a few thousand random points, squared and averaged. Initial and boundary values are further mean-square terms, each with a weight. Training is Adam first, then a short L-BFGS stage (a quasi-Newton optimiser that uses curvature estimated from recent gradients).

The rod's reference is a manufactured solution, T(x, t) = e^(−t) sin(πx) + x(1 − x): I pick the answer first and derive the source term from it, so any error is the network's own. An implicit finite-difference solver agrees with that field to 0.00025, which confirms the setup before any network is trained.

The inverse move: one trainable number

An inverse PINN differs from a forward one by a single registration: the unknown coefficient becomes a parameter the optimiser updates next to the weights, and a mean-square term over the sensor readings replaces the information the coefficient used to provide. Stage 2 does it in DeepXDE, Stage 3 by hand; the two columns below correspond one to one.

An operator instead of one solution

A PINN learns one solution; change a coefficient and it must be trained again. Stage 4 learns the operator that takes a whole permeability field to the pressure field of steady Darcy flow, −∇·(a∇u) = 1 on the unit square. A branch network reads the permeability at the 100 grid points, a trunk network reads a query location, and their dot product is the pressure there. The training pairs come from a finite-volume solver: two-material blocky fields with permeabilities 3 and 12, 1,500 for training and 80 held out.

Baselines and references

  • Exact or manufactured solutions where one exists (the rod, the plate, λ = 4 with sin(2x)), a finite-difference solver otherwise (the fuel pin, the square with k = 0.8).
  • For the operator, the finite-volume solver on 80 held-out fields, and four hand-drawn fields outside the training family.
  • For every trained result, three seeds (0, 1, 2), so each comparison is against the spread between repeats.

What decides whether it works

ReLU is the one setting that breaks the method: with it the maximum error is 3.175 against 0.018 with tanh. ReLU is piecewise linear, its second derivative is zero almost everywhere, so the diffusion term silently vanishes from the residual. More collocation points help with diminishing returns (0.037, 0.014, 0.006 for 300, 1,500, 3,000 points), but each of those is one run and inside the trainer's seed range.

  • Loss weights matter at the extremes. With weights 0.01 on the initial and boundary terms the RMSE is 0.234 ± 0.072 against 0.021 ± 0.026 at the default: the network satisfies the equation and ignores the data, which is another solution of the same equation. One tiny and one huge weight (0.01, 1000) give 0.557 ± 0.131.
  • A longer fuel-pin window is harder, not easier. Ten seconds instead of two raise the RMSE to 8.41 ± 2.68 K with the same budget; the residual loss stays two orders of magnitude higher.
  • Not resolved: the strength of the steady part of the field, more interior points on the plate (0.0138 ± 0.0169 with 6,000 against 0.0040 ± 0.0014 with 2,500, two of the draws much worse), and the L-BFGS polish on the fuel pin (0.306 ± 0.203 K without it against 0.253 ± 0.068 K).

The L-BFGS stage does the recovery. After 3,000 Adam iterations k was still 1.51 to 1.53 in every run; L-BFGS, running its 1,000 iterations, brought it to 0.81 to 0.87. An earlier execution on the Apple GPU gave k = 1.0585 with the same code and seed, because its L-BFGS stage stopped after 59 iterations. I now read the L-BFGS iteration count before I read the recovered value.

A low loss does not mean the unknown was found. The Poiseuille profile depends on viscosity and pressure gradient only through their ratio, so a doubled gradient gives a doubled viscosity (ratio 49.74 against 49.82). With no source term the residual multiplies k by a Laplacian the solution makes zero; the readings are fitted and k stays near its start.

The solidification front is the clean case: the coordinate ξ = x/s(t) freezes the moving boundary, and the front coefficient comes out at the exact value in all three seeds without any sensor readings, because the Stefan condition alone pins it.

Results 3 — The same inverse problem by hand (Stage 3)

With seeds 0, 1 and 2, λ ends at 3.909, 3.855 and 3.901: 3.888 ± 0.029, an error of 2.8 ± 0.7 %. Each run takes 2.8 s. Every run ends below 4 and the trajectory is still rising after 10,000 iterations; continuing for another 10,000 brings the unseeded run in the figure from 3.852 to 3.915. Another unseeded run, in the repository's notebook, reached 3.984 and 3.990: without a seed, a single number here is one draw.

Side by side with Stage 2, the inverse idea itself is two lines in either form. What decides the result is everything around them: the L-BFGS stage that DeepXDE has and this loop lacks, whether the readings determine the unknown, the seed and even the device.

Results 4 — A whole family: DeepONet for Darcy flow (Stage 4)

The first model reached a validation relative L2 error of 0.052. Three further initialisations on the same data give 0.0704 ± 0.0053, so 0.052 is a good draw, not the typical result. On a 16 × 16 grid a fresh model reaches 0.0840 ± 0.0030. The branch reads a fixed number of values, so a field on another grid cannot be fed to a trained model: changing the grid means a new branch and new data.

A learned operator gives no warning when asked about something it was not trained on; it still returns a field. A checkerboard finer than any training block gives 0.233 ± 0.080 and is predicted too low everywhere; a uniform field, which is just a block filling the square, gives 0.024 ± 0.002.

Key observations

  1. One recipe, three roles. The same loop of network, residual and optimiser solves a field to a few thousandths, recovers a coefficient to within a few per cent, and, with the residual replaced by solver pairs, answers a whole family of inputs to about 6 to 8 % error.
  2. The activation is a correctness choice, not a tuning choice. With ReLU the second derivative vanishes and the PINN fails (3.175 against 0.018); every network whose loss contains a derivative needs a smooth activation.
  3. In an inverse problem, the optimiser's second stage does the work. Adam alone left the conductivity at its starting value in every run; L-BFGS recovered it. A stage that stops after seconds leaves a leftover of the starting guess, and the same code gave 1.0585 on the GPU and 0.8466 on the CPU for that reason.
  4. A fitted loss is not an identified unknown. A ratio of two parameters and a field whose Laplacian is zero both fit the readings well and recover the wrong value; the readings decide identifiability, not the optimiser.
  5. Single runs mislead in both directions. The DeepONet's 0.052 is a good draw (0.0704 ± 0.0053); several effects in the PINN studies vanish inside the seed spread; two others, extreme loss weights and the long window, survive it.
  6. An operator pays for itself only inside its family and at volume. Error falls steadily with training data, rises sharply on fields unlike the training set, and on a small grid the break-even is tens of thousands of queries.

Conclusion

On problems with known answers, a network that is given the equation works: as a forward solver, as an inverse solver for a scalar coefficient, and as an operator over a family of inputs. The numbers that survive three seeds are the ones I would build on; the ones that do not are single runs, and a few of the most visible numbers in these notebooks were exactly that.

For a single forward problem the classical solver remains the default: it was the reference in every stage. A PINN earns its place when sparse, noisy readings have to be combined with an equation, and an operator when the same kind of problem is asked many times. What these stages do not establish is a comparison with the simplest classical alternative for the same task (a least-squares fit for λ, the finite-difference solver inside an optimiser for k, a linear map for the operator), a recovered value checked against held-out readings, or the operator's speed-up on a grid where the solver is slow. Those are the next steps, together with an L-BFGS stage for the hand-written loop and a nudge test of each recovered value.
