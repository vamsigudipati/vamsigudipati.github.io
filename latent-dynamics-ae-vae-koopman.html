Compress, structure, evolve: autoencoders, a VAE and a Koopman autoencoder on one wake flow

One thousand snapshots of the flow past two plates, 16,384 numbers each. A convolutional autoencoder keeps them in ten numbers with 0.44 of the fluctuation missed, against 0.76 for ten POD modes scored on the same snapshots, and a variational autoencoder does the same with a latent space that can be sampled. The third job fails: a Koopman autoencoder, a linear step learned in that latent space, is worse than repeating the last snapshot at one step, worse than the mean flow from the first step of a rollout on a held-out block, and unstable in two seeds out of three. DMD, a least-squares fit on ten POD coefficients, is the better forecast.

Objective

A flow field is thousands of numbers per snapshot, and most of them are redundant: the wake behind a body repeats itself, with variations. A reduced-order model lives on a handful of numbers instead. This project follows one dataset through the three jobs such a model has to do: compress each snapshot into a few numbers, give those numbers a structure that can be sampled, and evolve them in time. Every stage is measured against the linear method that does the same job without a neural network. The work is four stages, each a notebook:

  1. Stage 1 — Compressing a flow field: dense and convolutional autoencoders against POD. 1,000 snapshots of (u, v) on a 128 × 64 grid, 16,384 numbers each, compressed to 10 by a dense and a convolutional autoencoder, against proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) with 10 modes.
  2. Stage 2 — Structuring the latent space: latent size, trajectories and a variational autoencoder. How the error falls with the latent size from 2 to 50, what the latent coordinates trace over time, and a variational autoencoder (VAE) whose latent space is pulled towards a normal distribution so that it can be sampled.
  3. Stage 3 — Evolving the latent state: a Koopman autoencoder against persistence and DMD. An encoder, a 10 × 10 matrix and a decoder trained together so that one matrix multiplication steps the flow forward, against persistence and dynamic mode decomposition (DMD).
  4. Stage 4 — Latent size and rollouts: what the networks buy over POD and DMD. The same comparisons on equal terms: POD fitted on the training snapshots only and scored on the same validation set as the networks, and the rollouts on a block of time held out entirely, against DMD, persistence and the mean flow.

Every training is repeated with seeds 0, 1 and 2, seed 0 being the base run, and a difference counts as an effect only when it exceeds the seed spread. The dataset is a 2-D simulation of viscous flow past two collinear plates at Reynolds number 100.

Problem statement

Three questions, one per job. How much better than the best linear compression is a nonlinear one of the same size, and does the answer survive a change of seed? What does forcing the latent space towards a normal distribution cost in reconstruction, and what does it buy in sampling? And can a linear operator learned in that latent space forecast the flow at all, measured against the forecast that costs nothing (the last snapshot) and against DMD, the linear method that uses the same ten numbers?

Method

Compression against the linear reference

After the time mean is removed, POD by singular value decomposition gives the best linear basis of rank d: the d patterns that capture most of the snapshots' variance. The autoencoders learn a nonlinear map to d numbers and back, trained on mean squared error with Adam, early stopping after 20 flat epochs and the learning rate halved after 8. The error everywhere is the relative L2 error of the reconstruction, per snapshot and velocity component, averaged over the snapshots scored.

Stages 1 and 2 fit POD on all 1,000 snapshots and score it on all 1,000, while the autoencoders are scored on the 200 validation snapshots. That is how the notebooks were set up, and I keep their numbers on this page as stated; Stage 4 repeats the comparison with POD fitted on the 800 training snapshots and scored on the same 200.

A latent space with a distribution

A linear step in the latent space

The idea is Koopman's: a nonlinear system can look linear in the right coordinates, and the encoder is asked to find them. The eigenvalues of A then say how each latent direction decays or grows per step, and a rollout is one encode, n matrix products and one decode. DMD is the linear reference: project the snapshots on 10 POD modes and fit the least-squares matrix that steps those coefficients forward. Persistence predicts the last snapshot; the mean flow predicts zero fluctuation, which gives a relative error of exactly 1, the line that any forecast has to stay under.

Which device

Each network trains on its faster device, with one exception that the timing did not predict: Stage 1 runs entirely on the CPU, because with the GPU visible its convolutional autoencoder, although pinned to the CPU, ran at 6 seconds per epoch instead of 1 and would have passed my 45-minute limit per training. GPU trainings repeated to every printed digit for a given seed.

Ten POD modes miss three quarters of the fluctuation; ten latent numbers of an autoencoder miss less than half. The convolutional network does this with 12 times fewer weights, and a second block at a sixth of the epochs is lower again, though with a wider spread.

The error field shows where the ten numbers fall short: the fine structure near the plates and the smaller eddies downstream, which no ten-dimensional description keeps. What the autoencoder keeps better than POD is the placement and shape of the large structures.

What a latent coordinate means is the other finding of this stage. Decoding a 2 σ step along each latent direction gives a field, but those fields are sensitivities of the decoder, not modes: they do not add up to the snapshot and they are not orthogonal, so the ordered, additive structure of POD is the price of the lower error.

The prediction error is almost the reconstruction error: the operator adds only 0.006, so what the model loses, it loses in the encoder and decoder, not in the step. But a model that cannot beat the last snapshot is not a forecast, and the random split flatters it: 160 of its 200 validation targets are training inputs.

The reconstruction term of the loss dominates the training, and the two-and-a-half-fold gap between validation and training loss says the encoder and decoder overfit the 799 training pairs. A linear operator can only be as good as the coordinates it is given.

Two more things about this stage are worth stating. Standardising every grid point by its own standard deviation magnifies near-still points upstream of the plates by up to 20,000 times, which is a strange thing to ask a decoder to match. And the notebook's rollout compares its predictions with the first 50 snapshots, not with the true futures of its 50 starts, so its rollout curve measures nothing; Stage 4 does the rollout properly.

The Koopman autoencoder does not forecast this flow. On starts it has never seen it is worse than predicting the mean flow from the very first step, and in the three-seed mean it is worse than DMD at every horizon up to 50 steps (1.42 against 1.10 at 50); only seed 0 alone drops below DMD, from step 36, where both are already above 1. DMD, a least-squares fit on ten POD coefficients with no training, is the best forecast beyond two steps and stays below the mean-flow line for about 30.

The eigenvalues explain the growth. DMD's largest modulus is 0.998, so its rollout decays slowly towards the mean; the learned operator has eigenvalues outside the unit circle in two of three seeds, so those rollouts grow. Nothing in the training loss asks the operator to be stable, and a one-step loss on a random split does not reward it either.

Key observations

  1. Nonlinear compression beats linear compression, and the result survives the fair test. Ten latent numbers miss 0.44 to 0.50 of the fluctuation against POD's 0.757 on the same validation snapshots; the advantage holds from 5 to 50 latent numbers and in every seed.
  2. The convolutional autoencoder wins on error and on size, 0.437 against 0.465 with 12 times fewer weights, but most of those weights are still in a dense bottleneck, so its size is not independent of the resolution as the original notebook claims.
  3. A VAE at small β reconstructs as well as an autoencoder and generates poorly; at β = 1 its reconstruction is far worse (0.641) and whether its samples improve was not measured. The two uses pull the same knob in opposite directions, and three seeds were needed to see the β = 1 number move from 0.449 to 0.641.
  4. A learned linear step is not a forecast unless it beats the last snapshot. The Koopman autoencoder's one-step error (0.531) is above persistence (0.366) in every seed; on a held-out block it is above the mean flow from step 1 and above DMD at every horizon.
  5. Stability is a property of the seed, not of the method. One seed's operator has all eigenvalues inside the unit circle, two seeds' have some outside; the earlier run had one outside too. A method whose stability flips with the seed needs a constraint, not a lucky draw.
  6. The split is part of the result. With a random split, 190 of 200 validation snapshots have a training neighbour one step away and 160 of 200 forecast targets are training inputs. The compression result stands when POD is scored on the same held-out snapshots; the forecasting result reverses on a held-out block of time.

Conclusion

Compression works and forecasting does not. Ten numbers from an autoencoder describe a wake snapshot about twice as well as ten POD modes, the VAE adds a sampleable latent space at a small cost in reconstruction at β = 0.001, and both results hold when POD is fitted and scored on the same terms as the networks. The Koopman autoencoder, trained to step those numbers forward with a 10 × 10 matrix, loses to persistence at one step, to the mean flow and to DMD on a held-out block, and is unstable in two seeds out of three.

What the stages do not establish: whether a Koopman autoencoder with a stability constraint, a multi-step loss or a contiguous training split would forecast this flow, since the notebook's setup was kept as received; whether the VAE's generated fields improve with more snapshots or a β schedule; and how the autoencoders fare on a split in time rather than a random one, which Stage 4 applies only to the forecasts. The next steps are a Koopman model trained on a contiguous split with a multi-step loss and the operator's spectrum penalised outside the unit circle, the compression sweep repeated on the same contiguous split, and a longer dataset for the VAE.
